# N-006｜Two-Digit Rotation Multiples

## 题面

For each integer $n \ge 100$ we define $T(n)$ to be the number obtained from $n$ by moving the two leading digits to the end. For example, $T(12345) = 34512$ and $T(100) = 10$. Find all integers $n \ge 100$ for which:
$$
n + T(n) = 10n.
$$

## 答案

All n of the form n = 10(10^{6k} − 1)/91 for integers k ≥ 1; equivalently, n is the k-fold concatenation of the six-digit block 109890.

## 解法要点

The general solution is
$$
n = \frac{10}{91} \times (10^{6k} - 1) = (1 + 10^6 + 10^{12} + \dots + 10^{6(k-1)}) \times 109890
$$
for any positive integer $k$. In other words, the number $n$ consists of $k$ replicates of the digits 109890.

Let $m$ be the number of digits of $n$ so $m \ge 3$ when $n \ge 100$. Dividing $n$ by $10^{m-2}$, let $q$ be the quotient and $r$ the remainder. This splits the digits of $n$ into the first two and the remaining digits,
$$
n = 10^{m-2}q + r
$$
where $10 \le q \le 99$ and $0 \le r < 10^{m-2}$. Rearranging the digits we have:
$$
T(n) = 100r + q.
$$
The equation $n + T(n) = 10n$ is equivalent to $T(n) = 9n$ which we write in terms of $q$ and $r$:
$$
100r + q = 9 \times 10^{m-2}q + 9r
$$
or
$$
91r = (9 \times 10^{m-2} - 1)q. \qquad (7)
$$

We know that $q \ge 10$. We now claim that $q = 10$ for any solution. For a contradiction, suppose that $q \ge 11$. Then equation (7) implies that
$$
(9 \times 10^{m-2} - 1) \times 11 \le (9 \times 10^{m-2} - 1)q = 91r < 91 \times 10^{m-2}.
$$
Comparing the left and right end gives $(99-91) \times 10^{m-2} \le 11$, contradicting $m \ge 3$. Therefore $q = 10$. Substituting $q = 10$ into equation (7) gives
$$
91r = (9 \times 10^{m-2} - 1) \times 10.
$$
As $\gcd(91, 10) = 1$ then 91 must divide $9 \times 10^{m-2} - 1$. This is not only necessary but also sufficient, as we can then write:
$$
r = \frac{9 \times 10^{m-2} - 1}{91} \times 10 < \frac{9}{91} \times 10^{m-1} < 10^{m-2} \quad (8)
$$
and $r$ is then an integer that lies in the feasible range.
It remains then to find the values of $m$ for which $9 \times 10^{m-2} - 1$ is a multiple of 91. Equivalently, we require that $(9 + 91) \times 10^{m-2} - 1 = 10^m - 1$, is a multiple of 91. As $91 = 7 \times 13$ we can use a short cut to check that $10^m - 1$ is a multiple both of 7 and of 13. Remainders tabulated below:

| m | $10^m$ (mod 7) | $10^m$ (mod 13) | $10^m$ (mod 91) |
|---|----------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 0 | 1              | 1               | 1               |
| 1 | 3              | 10              | 10              |
| 2 | 2              | 9               | 9               |
| 3 | 6              | 12              | 90              |
| 4 | 4              | 3               | 81              |
| 5 | 5              | 4               | 82              |
| 6 | 1              | 1               | 1               |

The pattern continues with period 6. Therefore, $m$ must be a multiple of 6, and, as $m \ge 3$ we have $m = 6k$ for some positive integer $k$.
Reconstructing the pieces and using equation (8) we have:
$$
\begin{align*}
q &= 10 & r &= \frac{9 \times 10^{6k-2} - 1}{91} \times 10 \\
n &= 10^{6k-2}q + r = 10 \times \frac{91 \times 10^{6k-2} + 9 \times 10^{6k-2} - 1}{91} = 10 \times \frac{10^{6k} - 1}{91}.
\end{align*}
$$
We can write the digits explicitly, noting that:
$$
10 \times \frac{10^{6k} - 1}{91} = \frac{10 \times (10^6 - 1)}{91} (1 + 10^6 + 10^{12} + \dots + 10^{6(k-1)}).
$$
As $10 \times (10^6 - 1)/91 = 989010$ the stated representation follows.

---

The equation $n + T(n) = 10n$ could also be written as $T(n) = 9n$.
As $n$ and $T(n)$ have the same number of digits, the leading digit of $n$ must be equal to 1 and the leading digit of $T(n) = 9n$, which is the third digit from the left of $n$, has then to be equal to 9. This implies that there occurs a carry from the third to the second place from the left, hence the second digit of $n$ must be zero. The shapes of $n$ and $T(n)$ are therefore
$$
n = 109 **\ldots** * \qquad T(n) = 9 **\ldots** * 10
$$
where each star stands for a digit and the dots indicate that the number of digits of $n$ is currently unknown. We can now work our way backwards from $T(n) = 9n$ to $n$, starting at the end. The key feature we are using here is that $9k \equiv -k \equiv 10 - k \pmod{10}$.
Let the rightmost unknown digit be equal to $d$ so that we have
$$
n = 109 **\ldots** * d \qquad 9n = T(n) = 9 **\ldots** * d10
$$
then $9d \equiv 0 \pmod{10}$, hence $d = 0$. This gives us
$$
n = 109 **\ldots** * 0 \qquad 9n = T(n) = 9 **\ldots** * 010
$$
We will again call the rightmost unknown digit $d$ so that we have
$$
n = 109 **\ldots** * d0 \qquad 9n = T(n) = 9 **\ldots** * d010
$$
and $9d \equiv 1 \pmod{10}$, hence $d = 9$. We now need to check if this digit could already be the third digit from the left, i.e. $n = 1090$. But $1090 \cdot 9 = 9810 \neq T(1090)$. This gives us, with new $d$,
$$
n = 109 **\ldots** * d90 \qquad 9n = T(n) = 9 **\ldots** * d9010
$$
and, keeping the carry in mind, $9d + 8 \equiv 0 \pmod{10}$. Therefore, $d = 8$ and
$$
n = 109 **\ldots** * d890 \qquad 9n = T(n) = 9 **\ldots** * d89010
$$
with $9d + 8 \equiv 9 \pmod{10}$, which implies $d = 9$. We have to check again if this digit could already be the third digit from the left, i.e. $n = 109890$. Indeed, we now have $9n = 9 \cdot 109890 = 989010 = T(n)$.
So far we have shown that $109890$ is one solution and that the last six digits of $T(n)$ for any solution $n$ need to be $989010$. This implies that the last six digits of any solution $n$ are equal to $989010/9 = 109890$.
We could now continue in the manner above to see that this sequence of digits must repeat if $n$ has more than six digits. Alternatively, we could argue as follows.

Let $m$ be the number obtained from $n$ by removing the last six digits. We show that $T(m) = 9m$. Clearly, $T(m)$ agrees, except possible at the last two digits, with the number $T'(n)$ obtained from $T(n)$ by removing the last six digits. As we have seen above, the two leading digits of $n$, and so also of $m$, are 10. As the last six digits of $n$ are 109890, the last two digits of $T'(n)$ are 10. Hence, $T'(n) = T(m)$. Using $T(n) = 9n$ it follows now that $T(m) = 9m$. It follows by induction that $n$ has $6k$ digits ($k \ge 1$) and consists of $k$ consecutive blocks of six digits, all equal to 109890.

## 提示阶梯

1. 把十进制表示按前两位与其余各位拆开，分别表示原数和轮转后的数。重点关注位数带来的范围限制。
2. 设原数有 m 位，写成 n=10^(m-2)q+r，则 T(n)=100r+q。把条件化为 q、r 的一次关系，再利用 10≤q≤99 与 0≤r<10^(m-2) 压缩 q 的可能性。
3. 由范围比较先迫使 q 取最小的两位数，再把整除条件改写为某个两位模数整除 10^m-1。接下来计算 10 的幂在该模数下的循环，并据此重建 r。
